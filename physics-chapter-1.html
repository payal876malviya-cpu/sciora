Units and Measurements

Physics is built on measurement. Before we can describe motion, force or energy, we must agree on what to measure and how to write it. This chapter gives you that common language — units, dimensions, significant figures and errors.

🎯 Learning objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Explain what a physical quantity is and tell fundamental from derived quantities.
  • Use the SI system, its seven base units and common prefixes.
  • Count significant figures and round answers correctly.
  • Write the dimensional formula of any physical quantity.
  • Check equations, convert units and derive simple relations using dimensions.
  • Calculate absolute, relative and percentage errors, and combine errors.
  • Distinguish accuracy from precision.

1Physical Quantities

Physics is the science of measurement. Anything that can be measured is called a physical quantity. Length, mass, time, speed, temperature and force are all physical quantities. Feelings like love or anger are not, because we cannot measure them with an instrument.

Every measurement has two parts

Unit (u)cm

If someone says “my desk is 120”, you cannot tell if it is 120 cm, 120 inches or 120 m. The unit gives the number its meaning.

Bigger unit → smaller number. The same length can be written as 1.2 m or 120 cm or 1200 mm. The length did not change — only the unit did. In symbols: n₁u₁ = n₂u₂.

Two kinds of physical quantities

Scalars

Only size (magnitude). Examples: mass, time, temperature, speed.

Vectors

Size and direction. Examples: displacement, velocity, force.

Common mistake: Writing an answer without a unit. In exams, “5” is wrong but “5 m s^-1” is right.

2Fundamental and Derived Units

Fundamental (base) quantities are independent — you cannot get them from other quantities. Their units are fundamental units. Derived quantities are made by combining base quantities, so their units are derived units.

Think of it like language: base quantities are the letters; derived quantities are the words made from them.

The 7 base quantities

Length, mass, time, electric current, thermodynamic temperature, amount of substance and luminous intensity.

4Significant Figures

No instrument is perfect. Significant figures are the digits we are sure about, plus one last uncertain digit. They show how reliable a measurement is.

The 5 rules

  1. All non-zero digits are significant. (2.345 → 4)
  2. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant. (1005 → 4)
  3. Leading zeros are not significant — they only show the decimal place. (0.0025 → 2)
  4. Trailing zeros without a decimal point are not significant. (1500 → 2)
  5. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant. (2.50 → 3, 0.0250 → 3)
Scientific notation removes confusion. 4.700 × 10^2 has 4 significant figures. The power of ten never counts. Also, changing units does not change significant figures: 2.308 cm = 0.02308 m (both 4).

Try it yourself

Rounding off

  • Digit to drop is less than 5 → keep the previous digit. (3.42 → 3.4)
  • Digit to drop is more than 5 → add 1 to the previous digit. (3.46 → 3.5)
  • Digit is exactly 5 (nothing after it) → make the previous digit even. (3.45 → 3.4, 3.35 → 3.4)

Using significant figures in calculations

Add / subtract

Answer keeps as many decimal places as the number with the fewest decimal places.
2.5 + 3.27 = 5.77 → 5.8

Multiply / divide

Answer keeps as many significant figures as the number with the fewest significant figures.
4.5 × 2.30 = 10.35 → 10

Exact numbers (like the 2 in 2πr, or counting 5 students) have unlimited significant figures and never limit the answer.

5Dimensions and Dimensional Formulae

The dimensions of a physical quantity tell us which base quantities it is made of, and with what powers. We write base dimensions in square brackets:

LengthMassTimeCurrentTemperatureAmountLuminous int.
[L][M][T][A][K][mol][cd]

The expression showing these powers is the dimensional formula, e.g. [M^a L^b T^c].

How to find it — 3 easy steps

  1. Write the formula of the quantity.
  2. Replace each quantity by its dimensions.
  3. Simplify with exponent rules (dividing → negative power).
Example — Force:
Force = mass × acceleration
= [M] × [L T^-2]
= [M L T^-2]

Speed

distance ÷ time = [L] ÷ [T] = [L T^-1]

Work

force × distance = [M L T^-2][L] = [M L^2 T^-2]

Dimensionless quantities

Ratios of two similar quantities have no dimensions: angle, strain, refractive index, relative density, trigonometric ratios, and pure numbers like π. Their dimensional formula is [M^0 L^0 T^0].

Remember: Dimensions tell what a quantity is made of, not its size. Torque and work both have [M L^2 T^-2], yet they are different quantities.

6Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis uses dimensions as a quick checking tool. It rests on one idea:

Principle of Homogeneity: In a correct equation, every term on both sides must have the same dimensions. You can only add or subtract quantities of the same kind (you cannot add metres to seconds).

Use 1 — Check an equation

Is v = u + at correct? Dimensions: [v] = [L T^-1], [u] = [L T^-1], [at] = [L T^-2][T] = [L T^-1]. All terms match ✔ — so it is dimensionally correct.

Use 2 — Convert units

n₂ = n₁ [M₁/M₂]^a [L₁/L₂]^b [T₁/T₂]^c

For a quantity with dimensions [M^a L^b T^c]. Example: 1 N = ? dyne. Force is [M L T^-2], so n₂ = 1 × (1000 g / 1 g)^1 × (100 cm / 1 cm)^1 = 10^5 dyne.

Use 3 — Derive a relation

Suppose the time period T of a pendulum depends on length l, mass m and gravity g. Write T ∝ l^a m^b g^c, match dimensions and solve. (Full steps are in Solved Numericals, problem 4.) The result: T = k √(l/g).

Limitations

  • It cannot give dimensionless constants (like ½ or 2π).
  • It cannot handle sin, cos, log or exponential functions.
  • It does not say whether a quantity is a scalar or a vector.
  • It is hard to use when a quantity depends on more than three others.
  • A dimensionally correct equation may still be physically wrong. For example, K = mv² is dimensionally fine, but the true formula is K = ½mv².

7Errors in Measurement

No measurement is perfectly exact. The difference between the measured value and the true value is called the error.

Types of errors

🔧 Systematic

Always pushes the result the same way. Causes: faulty instrument (zero error), wrong method, personal habit. Fix: calibrate, correct the method.

🎲 Random

Changes unpredictably up or down. Causes: small changes in surroundings, tiny differences in reading. Fix: take many readings and average.

⚠️ Gross

Human mistakes: misreading the scale, writing wrong data. Fix: be careful and repeat.

Least count = the smallest value an instrument can measure (ruler 1 mm, vernier callipers usually 0.1 mm, screw gauge usually 0.01 mm). The least count error is the error due to this limit.

Calculating errors from repeated readings

Take readings a₁, a₂, … aₙ.

  1. Mean value a_mean = (a₁ + a₂ + … + aₙ) ÷ n
  2. Absolute error of each reading: Δaᵢ = |a_mean − aᵢ|
  3. Mean absolute error Δa_mean = (Δa₁ + Δa₂ + … + Δaₙ) ÷ n
  4. Relative error = Δa_mean ÷ a_mean
  5. Percentage error = relative error × 100 %

The result is written as a = a_mean ± Δa_mean.

Combining errors

CalculationError rule
Z = A + B or Z = A − BΔZ = ΔA + ΔB (absolute errors add)
Z = A × B or Z = A ÷ BΔZ/Z = ΔA/A + ΔB/B (relative errors add)
Z = A^nΔZ/Z = n × ΔA/A
Z = A^p B^q ÷ C^rΔZ/Z = p ΔA/A + q ΔB/B + r ΔC/C
Never subtract errors. Even for Z = A − B, the errors add — in the worst case both errors push in opposite directions.

8Accuracy and Precision

🎯 Accuracy

How close a measurement is to the true value.

📍 Precision

How close repeated measurements are to each other (how fine the instrument is).

Example: a weighing machine

True mass = 50.0 g.

  • Readings 52.1, 52.0, 52.2 g → very close to each other (precise) but far from 50.0 (not accurate). Likely a zero error.
  • Readings 49.8, 50.3, 50.1 g → close to 50.0 (accurate) and fairly close to each other.
AccuracyPrecision
Compares withTrue valueOther readings
Improved byCalibrating the instrumentFiner instrument, careful repeated readings
Linked toSystematic errorRandom error and least count
Memory trick: Accurate = Aimed at the truth. Precise = Repeatable.

∑ Formula Section

Tap through the key formulas, each with a short explanation and example.

Physical quantity

Q = n × u

n is the number, u is the unit. If the unit becomes bigger, n becomes smaller: n₁u₁ = n₂u₂.

Example: 2 m = 200 cm, since 2 × 1 m = 200 × 1 cm.

Unit conversion by dimensions

n₂ = n₁ [M₁/M₂]^a [L₁/L₂]^b [T₁/T₂]^c

Use it when the quantity has dimensions [M^a L^b T^c].

Example: 1 J = 10^7 erg (work is [M L^2 T^-2]: 1000 × 100^2 = 10^7).

Significant figures in calculations

Add / subtract → fewest decimal places
Multiply / divide → fewest significant figures

Example: 12.11 + 18.0 + 1.012 = 31.122 → 31.1.

Mean and absolute error

a_mean = Σaᵢ / n    Δaᵢ = |a_mean − aᵢ|    Δa_mean = ΣΔaᵢ / n

Average of readings, how far each reading is from it, and the average of those gaps.

Relative and percentage error

Relative error = Δa_mean / a_mean    % error = (Δa_mean / a_mean) × 100

Example: a = 50 cm, Δa = 1 cm → relative error 0.02, percentage error 2%.

Combination of errors

Z = A ± B → ΔZ = ΔA + ΔB
Z = A × B or A / B → ΔZ/Z = ΔA/A + ΔB/B
Z = A^n → ΔZ/Z = n·ΔA/A

Example: Volume of a cube V = a^3. If a has 2% error, V has 3 × 2 = 6% error.

Least count

Vernier: LC = 1 MSD − 1 VSD
Screw gauge: LC = pitch ÷ number of circular scale divisions

Example: pitch 1 mm, 100 divisions → LC = 0.01 mm.

Principle of homogeneity

[LHS] = [RHS]

Each term of a correct equation has the same dimensions.

Example: s = ut + ½at^2 → every term is [L] ✔.

✏️ Solved Numericals

Try each problem yourself first, then open the solution.

1. Convert 72 km/h into m/s.
  1. 1 km = 1000 m and 1 hour = 3600 s.
  2. 72 km/h = 72 × 1000 m ÷ 3600 s.
  3. = 72000 ÷ 3600 = 20.
72 km/h = 20 m s^-1
Shortcut: km/h → m/s: multiply by 5/18.
2. Find the dimensional formula of pressure.
  1. Pressure = Force ÷ Area.
  2. [Force] = [M L T^-2], [Area] = [L^2].
  3. [Pressure] = [M L T^-2] ÷ [L^2] = [M L^(1−2) T^-2].
[Pressure] = [M L^-1 T^-2]
3. Check whether s = ut + ½at² is dimensionally correct.
  1. LHS: [s] = [L].
  2. First term: [ut] = [L T^-1][T] = [L].
  3. Second term: [½at²] = [L T^-2][T^2] = [L]. (The ½ has no dimensions.)
  4. Every term is [L].
Yes — dimensionally correct.
4. Derive the time period of a simple pendulum using dimensions.
  1. Assume T depends on length l, mass m and gravity g: T = k l^a m^b g^c (k is a constant).
  2. Dimensions: [T] = [L]^a [M]^b [L T^-2]^c = [M^b L^(a+c) T^(−2c)].
  3. Compare with [T] = [M^0 L^0 T^1]:
    Mass: b = 0
    Length: a + c = 0
    Time: −2c = 1 → c = −½
  4. Then a = −c = +½.
  5. So T = k l^½ g^-½.
T = k √(l/g) (experiment shows k = 2π). Mass does not matter.
5. Using significant figures: (a) 12.11 + 18.0 + 1.012 (b) 4.5 cm × 2.30 cm

(a) Addition

  1. Add: 12.11 + 18.0 + 1.012 = 31.122.
  2. Decimal places: 2, 1, 3. The fewest is 1 (from 18.0).
  3. Round 31.122 to 1 decimal place → 31.1.

(b) Multiplication

  1. 4.5 × 2.30 = 10.35.
  2. Significant figures: 4.5 has 2, 2.30 has 3. The fewest is 2.
  3. Round 10.35 to 2 significant figures → 10 (written as 1.0 × 10^1).
(a) 31.1    (b) 10 cm²
6. A stopwatch gives these times for 20 oscillations: 2.63, 2.56, 2.42, 2.71, 2.80 s. Find the mean absolute error and percentage error.
  1. Mean = (2.63 + 2.56 + 2.42 + 2.71 + 2.80) ÷ 5 = 13.12 ÷ 5 = 2.624 ≈ 2.62 s.
  2. Absolute errors: 0.01, 0.06, 0.20, 0.09, 0.18.
  3. Mean absolute error = (0.01 + 0.06 + 0.20 + 0.09 + 0.18) ÷ 5 = 0.54 ÷ 5 = 0.108 ≈ 0.11 s.
  4. Relative error = 0.11 ÷ 2.62 ≈ 0.042.
  5. Percentage error = 0.042 × 100 ≈ 4.2%.
t = 2.62 ± 0.11 s, percentage error ≈ 4%
7. Length l = 10.0 ± 0.1 cm and breadth b = 5.0 ± 0.1 cm. Find the area with its error.
  1. Area A = l × b = 10.0 × 5.0 = 50 cm².
  2. For a product, relative errors add:
    ΔA/A = Δl/l + Δb/b = 0.1/10.0 + 0.1/5.0 = 0.01 + 0.02 = 0.03.
  3. ΔA = 0.03 × 50 = 1.5 cm².
A = 50 ± 1.5 cm² (3% error)
8. The side of a cube is measured as 2.0 ± 0.1 cm. Find the percentage error in its volume.
  1. V = a^3, so ΔV/V = 3 × Δa/a.
  2. Δa/a = 0.1/2.0 = 0.05 = 5%.
  3. ΔV/V = 3 × 5% = 15%.
  4. V = 2.0^3 = 8.0 cm^3, so ΔV = 0.15 × 8.0 = 1.2 cm^3.
Error in volume = 15% (V = 8.0 ± 1.2 cm³)

📝 Revision Notes

Tap a topic to open its notes.

1. Physical quantities
  • Physical quantity = anything measurable.
  • Measurement = number × unit.
  • Bigger unit → smaller number (n₁u₁ = n₂u₂).
  • Scalar: magnitude only. Vector: magnitude + direction.
2. Fundamental and derived units
  • 7 base quantities: length, mass, time, current, temperature, amount of substance, luminous intensity.
  • Derived quantities come from combining base ones (speed, force, density…).
  • Radian and steradian are dimensionless derived units.
3. SI system
  • SI base units: m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd.
  • Prefixes: kilo 10^3, mega 10^6, giga 10^9, milli 10^-3, micro 10^-6, nano 10^-9, pico 10^-12.
  • 1 light year = 9.46 × 10^15 m; 1 Å = 10^-10 m; 1 u = 1.66 × 10^-27 kg.
4. Significant figures
  • Non-zero digits and zeros between them count.
  • Leading zeros never count.
  • Trailing zeros count only after a decimal point.
  • Add/subtract → decimal places. Multiply/divide → significant figures.
  • Exact numbers have unlimited significant figures.
5. Dimensions
  • Dimensions = powers of base quantities, written [M^a L^b T^c].
  • Force [M L T^-2], work [M L^2 T^-2], pressure [M L^-1 T^-2].
  • Ratios, angles and pure numbers are dimensionless.
6. Dimensional analysis
  • Principle of homogeneity: all terms have equal dimensions.
  • Uses: check equations, convert units, derive relations.
  • Limits: no constants like ½ or 2π, no trig/log/exp, correct dimensions ≠ correct physics.
7. Errors
  • Types: systematic, random, gross.
  • Absolute error = |mean − reading|. Relative = Δa/a. Percentage = relative × 100.
  • Sum/difference: add absolute errors. Product/quotient: add relative errors. Power n: multiply relative error by n.
8. Accuracy and precision
  • Accuracy: closeness to true value. Precision: closeness of readings to each other.
  • Precise does not mean accurate.

  • SI base units: metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, candela.
  • Significant figures: 1005 → 4 · 0.0025 → 2 · 2.50 → 3 · 1500 → 2.
